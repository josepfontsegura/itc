Final exam 2026

Problem 1. A source sends an information bit that is $0$ with probability $\tfrac34$. The line is noisy. A sent $0$ arrives as a $1$ with probability $\tfrac15$, while a sent $1$ always arrives correctly. Given that a $1$ arrives, what is the probability that a $1$ was sent?

(a) $\tfrac14$
(b) $\tfrac25$
(c) $\tfrac58$
(d) $\tfrac38$

Show solution

The correct answer is (c) ★

By total probability, a $1$ arrives with probability $\tfrac34\cdot\tfrac15+\tfrac14\cdot1=\tfrac{3}{20}+\tfrac14=\tfrac25$. By Bayes' rule, the probability that a $1$ was sent given that a $1$ arrives is $\dfrac{\tfrac14\cdot1}{\tfrac25}=\tfrac58$.

Problem 2. An information bit is $1$ with probability $p$. What bound does Chebyshev's inequality give for the probability that the bit differs from its mean by at least one half?

(a) $4p(1-p)$
(b) $2p(1-p)$
(c) $p(1-p)$
(d) $4p$

Show solution

The correct answer is (a) ★

The bit is $0$ with probability $1-p$, so its mean is $p$ and its variance is $p(1-p)$. Chebyshev's inequality gives $\Pr\bigl(|X-p|\ge\tfrac12\bigr)\le\dfrac{p(1-p)}{(1/2)^2}=4p(1-p)$.

Problem 3. A prefix-free binary code has four codewords. Three of them are $0$, $10$ and $110$. The fourth codeword is as short as possible. The four symbols, in this order, have probabilities $\tfrac25$, $\tfrac15$, $\tfrac15$ and $\tfrac15$. What is the efficiency of this code?

(a) $1$
(b) $\tfrac{5\log_2(5)-2}{10}$
(c) $\tfrac{5\log_2(5)-2}{9}$
(d) $\tfrac{5\log_2(5)-2}{11}$

Show solution

The correct answer is (b) ★

The fourth codeword cannot start with $0$, $10$ or $110$, and $11$ is a prefix of $110$, so the shortest possible codeword is $111$, of length $3$. The average length is $1\cdot\tfrac25+2\cdot\tfrac15+3\cdot\tfrac15+3\cdot\tfrac15=2$, and the entropy is $-\tfrac25\log_2(\tfrac25)-\tfrac35\log_2(\tfrac15)=\log_2(5)-\tfrac25$. The efficiency is the entropy divided by the average length, $\tfrac{5\log_2(5)-2}{10}$.

Problem 4. A source emits, independently, one of two letters: the first with probability $\tfrac34$ and the second with probability $\tfrac14$. To compress it, the letters are grouped in pairs and an optimal prefix-free binary code is built for these pairs. How many bits per letter are used on average?

(a) $\tfrac{31}{32}$
(b) $1$
(c) $2-\tfrac34\log_2(3)$
(d) $\tfrac{27}{32}$

Show solution

The correct answer is (d) ★

The four pairs have probabilities $\tfrac{9}{16}$, $\tfrac{3}{16}$, $\tfrac{3}{16}$ and $\tfrac1{16}$. The optimal (Huffman) code has lengths $1$, $2$, $3$ and $3$, so its average length per pair is $1\cdot\tfrac9{16}+2\cdot\tfrac3{16}+3\cdot\tfrac3{16}+3\cdot\tfrac1{16}=\tfrac{27}{16}$. Per letter this is $\tfrac{27}{32}$, below the $1$ bit per letter of single-letter coding and above the entropy $2-\tfrac34\log_2(3)$. The suboptimal Shannon code (lengths $\lceil-\log_2 p\rceil$) would give $\tfrac{31}{32}$.

Problem 5. A single information bit, equally likely to be $0$ or $1$, is protected using a repetition code of rate $\tfrac13$. The receiver decodes by majority vote. The channel acts on each bit independently and never changes a $0$, but changes a $1$ into a $0$ with probability $p$, where $0<p<1$. What is the probability that the received word differs from the transmitted codeword but is still decoded correctly?

(a) $\tfrac{3p(1-p)^2}{2}$
(b) $3p(1-p)^2$
(c) $\tfrac{3p^2(1-p)}{2}$
(d) $\tfrac{p^3}{2}$

Show solution

The correct answer is (a) ★

The two codewords are $000$ and $111$. If $000$ is sent, no bit can change, so the received word never differs from it. If $111$ is sent, the received word differs but is still decoded as $111$ exactly when one single $1$ becomes $0$ (the other two keep the majority), with probability $3p(1-p)^2$. Averaging over the two equally likely codewords gives $\tfrac{3p(1-p)^2}{2}$.

Problem 6. A binary linear code has parity-check matrix $\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\end{pmatrix}$. Its codewords are sent with equal probability over a channel that flips each bit independently with probability $p$, where $0<p<\tfrac12$. What is the probability of receiving the all-zero sequence?

(a) $\tfrac18$
(b) $(1-p)^3$
(c) $\tfrac{(1-p)^3+3p^2(1-p)}{4}$
(d) $\tfrac{(1-p)^3+p^3}{2}$

Show solution

The correct answer is (c) ★

The codewords are the $3$-bit words with an even number of ones: $000$, $011$, $101$ and $110$ (weights $0$, $2$, $2$, $2$). To receive $000$ from a codeword of weight $w$, its $w$ ones must flip and its $3-w$ zeros must not, with probability $p^w(1-p)^{3-w}$. Averaging over the four equally likely codewords gives $\tfrac{(1-p)^3+3p^2(1-p)}{4}$.

Problem 7. A number $X$ is chosen at random, with equal probability, from $\{1,2,3\}$. A second number $Y$ is $1$ when $X$ is $3$, and $0$ otherwise. What is the mutual information between $X$ and $Y$, in bits?

(a) $\log_2(3)$
(b) $\log_2(3)-\tfrac23$
(c) $0$
(d) $\tfrac13\log_2(3)$

Show solution

The correct answer is (b) ★

$Y$ is a function of $X$, so knowing $X$ removes all uncertainty about $Y$; therefore the information equals the entropy of $Y$. Since $Y$ is $1$ with probability $\tfrac13$ and $0$ with probability $\tfrac23$, this entropy is $-\tfrac13\log_2\tfrac13-\tfrac23\log_2\tfrac23=\log_2(3)-\tfrac23$.
